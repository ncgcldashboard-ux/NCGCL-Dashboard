Exhibit F-7a · Cost-to-Income Ratio — World Bank Standard Methodology
Definition, Precedent & Why Finance Cost Is Adjusted Out of Total Income
World Bank Global Financial Development Database, Indicator GFDD.EI.07 — "Bank cost to income ratio (%)"
World Bank definition: "Operating expenses of a bank as a share of the sum of net-interest revenue and other operating income." Formally, C/I = Operating Expenses ÷ (Net Interest Revenue + Other Operating Income), where Net Interest Revenue = Interest/Investment Income − Interest/Finance Expense. The World Bank's underlying Bankscope/Orbis formula is expressed as data2090 ÷ (data2080 + data2085), where data2080 is net — not gross — interest revenue.

Why interest/finance expense is netted out of the denominator, not left in as part of "total income": for a bank or a financial institution such as NCGCL, interest expense is not a discretionary overhead comparable to rent or salaries — it is the direct cost of the funding used to generate interest and investment income in the first place. A bank that borrows PKR 100 at 10% to invest at 12% has "grossed up" both its income and its cost base by the same borrowing; measuring efficiency against gross income would flatter an institution simply for leveraging up, and would penalise an unleveraged institution that generates the same net income more efficiently. Netting the funding cost from the income base isolates the revenue the institution's operations actually retain to cover its operating expenses, making cost efficiency comparable on an apples-to-apples basis across institutions with different leverage — which is precisely the World Bank's rationale for defining the ratio this way, and why banking regulators (including the State Bank of Pakistan, in its Financial Soundness Indicator framework) follow the same net-income convention.

NCGCL calculation, YTD August 2026:
Operating (Admin) Expenses = PKR 157.04 Mn
Net Investment Income = Other Income PKR 629.99 Mn − Finance Cost PKR 88.05 Mn = PKR 541.94 Mn
+ Guarantee Revenue PKR 1.20 Mn = PKR 543.13 Mn (denominator)
C/I (WB Standard) = PKR 157.04 Mn ÷ PKR 543.13 Mn = 28.91%

NCGCL's existing "Un-Levered" C/I metric (28.98%) is already directionally consistent with this approach; the ~7 bps gap reflects the guarantee-revenue treatment noted above. Source: World Bank DataBank Metadata Glossary, GFDD.EI.07.

07 · Pakistan's Housing Challenge
Published
Housing / Policy · Aug 2026
Integrates the 2023 Digital Census, provincial housing-quality data, construction cost models, mortgage statistics and ten-country benchmarking into a single framework covering supply deficit, quality crisis, affordability gap and financing failure.

Key insights: (1) annual formal housing supply (~150,000 units) runs at ~18% of the ~840,000 units/year needed just to absorb population growth — a 5.6x deficit that is widening; (2) Pakistan's mortgage-to-GDP ratio of ~0.25–0.5% is the lowest in South Asia (India 6–11%, Malaysia 44%), a structural rather than cyclical problem; (3) the last large government housing programme (Naya Pakistan Housing, 5m-unit target) delivered only ~21,980 units — a 0.44% delivery rate — arguing for a differentiated, tiered instrument by province rather than one uniform subsidy.

09 · Crop-Level Actuarial Premium Estimation (AYII)
Published
Agriculture / Insurance · Aug 2026
Technical working paper estimating Area Yield Index Insurance premiums for wheat, rice, maize and cotton using district yield histories, daily weather data and forward climate-shock scenarios; explicitly framed as planning indications, not filed tariffs.

Key insights: (1) at 80% coverage, base rates are wheat 4.74%, rice 5.12%, spring maize 5.30%, autumn maize 5.58% and cotton 8.16%; (2) under extreme compound climate stress, cotton rises to 9.39% and rice to 5.55% — the paper separates historical climate already embedded in yield data from incremental future risk; (3) historical Crop Loan Insurance Scheme data (2008–2022) shows PKR 14.3 Bn of government premium against PKR 6.5 Bn of claims (a 45.5% claims ratio), providing a real-world anchor for the model.

10 · Pakistan DAP Market Economics & Subsidy Design
Published
Agriculture / Policy · Aug 2026
Analyses Pakistan's DAP fertiliser price cycle (Jul 2014–Mar 2026), replacing the generic retail-vs-international benchmark with a CIF landed-price bridge to isolate true seller contribution and estimate seasonal demand elasticity.

Key insights: (1) the local price premium over international benchmarks materially overstates seller profit — the post-distribution contribution is only ~15–16% of gross retail price once GST, port handling, financing and distribution costs are deducted; (2) October–November planting-window demand elasticity (−1.20 after excluding genuinely supply-constrained months) is far larger than the full-year average (−0.6 to −0.8), meaning a time-bound Rabi-season subsidy would move far more volume per rupee than a flat annual one; (3) aggregate data are too confounded by area, rainfall and crop mix to validate specific claims of a fixed wheat-output loss from reduced DAP use.

11 · Pakistan and the 2026–27 El Niño
Live Dashboard
Climate / Macro · NCGCL Research · Evidence cut 23-Aug-2026
Interactive dashboard assessing what a building "very strong" El Niño means for Pakistan's wheat, cotton, rice, maize and sugarcane, for the food import bill, and for domestic food prices — framed explicitly as a risk to hedge, not a forecast of crop failure.

Key insights: (1) NOAA puts the chance of a very strong event at >90%, peaking around the Kharif-to-Rabi transition in late 2026 and touching cotton/rice/maize, the full wheat season, and spring maize pollination heat risk; (2) history is mixed, not uniformly bad — 1997-98 (one of the strongest El Niños on record) saw wheat +12% and sugarcane +26%, while 2015-16 cotton losses (−29%) stemmed from a mix of rain timing, pests and reduced input spending, not drought alone; (3) recommended response is five-part optionality — watch weather, ensure timely planting, verify food stocks, line up suppliers/credit, and use insurance to protect farmer cash flow — rather than pre-emptive importing.

12 · Agricultural Storage Infrastructure Finance Facility (ASIFF)
Concept Stage
Agriculture / Infrastructure Finance · NCGCL Research · Prepared for IFC · 2026
A catalytic risk-sharing facility for accredited agricultural warehousing in Pakistan. Treats commercial storage as a distinct infrastructure-financing gap from smallholder harvest-liquidity needs (addressed separately by WLPSF), targeting warehouse operators, traders, millers, processors and exporters rather than farmers directly.

Key insights: (1) Phase I anchors on a wheat-led 1.0 MMT accredited portfolio (~67 standard 15,000-ton warehouses) concentrated in Punjab/Sindh surplus corridors — concentration over coverage, to build standardized commodity pools an EWR market can trade; (2) risk flows PFI → NCGCL → IFC: NCGCL issues the capex guarantee directly to PFIs (25% of portfolio), with an unfunded IFC counter-guarantee reimbursing NCGCL 75% of every payout from the first rupee — a reinsurance structure, not a junior-capital stack; (3) fixed-rate lending (5yr PKRV + 175–225 bps) is paired with a pre-funded Markup Stabilization Reserve that buys down the PFI's rate differential once KIBOR exceeds the fixed rate by 300 bps for three months, since floating-rate KIBOR debt is structurally incompatible with a 7-year warehouse asset.
