How Pakistan's Crop Insurance Premium Is Actually Estimated

A first-principles actuarial walk-through of Area Yield Index Insurance pricing for wheat, rice, maize and cotton — built from district yield histories, no-look-ahead expected-yield forecasting, historical burn-cost analysis, and a governed forward climate-shock overlay. Every figure on this page is drawn directly from the working paper and its actuarial workbook. These are technical planning indications, not filed tariffs or insurer quotations.

Central finding

The project does not support one actuarial premium for all major crops. Cotton's 8.16% historical/current rate sits 2.16 percentage points above the 6% comparator — about PKR 2,914 more per acre than a flat rate would collect. Rice and both maize seasons sit below 6% by 0.42–0.88 points, and the retained wheat benchmark sits 1.26 points below. A single blended rate quietly overprices three crop modules while underpricing the one with the largest and most climate-sensitive exposure.

Methodology

How Premiums Are Built

The project uses historical burn analysis as its central actuarial method: every historical crop year is converted into an AYII payout ratio, and the mean payout ratio becomes the pure loss cost. Three controls are then layered on top before the model produces a planning indication.

1
Raw district yield
Official Punjab Crop Reporting Service district yield histories — 20 to 40 crop years per district, depending on the module. No synthetic values are used.
2
Pre-season expected yield
One of three no-look-ahead methods (M3-5, OLS5, OLS7) selected purely on lowest forecast error — never on which one produces the lowest premium.
3
AYII trigger & payout
A threshold yield is set as coverage × expected yield. Actual yield below that threshold triggers a proportional, capped indemnity.
4
Historical burn cost
The mean payout ratio across all valid crop years becomes the pure loss cost — then loaded against a floor and blended with a retained prior by credibility.
5
Acreage-weighted rate
District rates are weighted by 2023–24 planted area; the V11 crop prior is retained on the Punjab area the model does not yet cover.

Method definitions
M3-5 — average of the middle three yields from the five immediately preceding valid crop years. Robust to one high/low outlier; can lag structural shifts.
OLS5 — log-linear trend fit to the five preceding years; one-year-ahead forecast. Responsive to recent change; can extrapolate sharply.
OLS7 — log-linear trend fit to the seven preceding years. A smoother alternative; still regime-sensitive.
Why detrending matters

Historical yield can rise over time as technology and farm practice change, so a simple long-run average can understate current expected yield after a structural improvement. But a full-sample trend line would use future information to score an earlier crop year — exactly the look-ahead bias the model is built to avoid. Maize shows why this matters most: the project records a structural yield step-up in the mid-2000s, and different admissible expected-yield methods produce materially different 80% burn costs for Nankana Sahib as a result. The model reports this spread as model risk rather than hiding it behind one point estimate.

V12.2 Climate-Shock Overlay

Historical area yield already contains realized heat, rainfall, flood and pest shocks — so V12.2 starts from the V12.1 historical rate rather than adding a second charge for climate that already happened. It assigns each crop module a residual heat and flood expected-loss budget; the historical/current state sets both multipliers to 1.0 and produces zero incremental charge. Only forward deterioration above that reference state is priced.

Section 6 & 7

Policy & Comparison

A crop priced below its risk indication collects less premium than the model expects for that risk; if farmers or lenders can choose which crops enter the scheme, higher-risk crops can become a larger share of the insured portfolio. A crop priced above its indication does the reverse — it can weaken uptake, or raise fiscal cost without improving protection, if government funds the gap.

Underpricing risk — cotton

If the scheme keeps a common 6% technical price, cotton's 2.16-point gap must be funded from somewhere: a future deficit, a cross-subsidy from lower-risk crops, a government top-up, or a lower level of risk transfer. The project does not estimate an adverse-selection coefficient — the policy concern follows directly from the pricing gap itself.

Overpricing risk — rice, maize, wheat

For rice the 6% comparator exceeds the model by about PKR 1,095/acre; for spring maize about PKR 808/acre; for wheat about PKR 1,274/acre against the base climate benchmark. If the farmer pays part of the premium, overpricing can weaken uptake; if government pays the difference, it raises fiscal cost without improving underlying protection.

Replace a flat crop-blended rate with a pricing ladder

Section 7.2 — one product architecture, more than one price

1
Crop-specific rate — the minimum level of differentiation
Replace the single blended rate with wheat / rice / spring maize / autumn maize / cotton rates, each reflecting its own historical burn and evidence quality.
2
District rates where the data are credible
Use district-level rates wherever yield history, boundaries and exposure are stable — cotton is the strongest current candidate, with five districts covering ~60% of Punjab cotton area.
3
Tehsil or smaller units — only once credible
Move to finer geography only after the local history meets the project's credibility standard; forcing granularity onto thin or unstable histories increases sampling error rather than reducing basis risk.
4
Keep a parent-district or crop prior when data are thin
Retain the V11 prior on unmodeled area rather than forcing a narrow sample to represent a full provincial portfolio — this is exactly what the acreage-weighted bridge does today.
Separate the actuarial price from subsidy policy
Section 7.1 & 4.9 — the commercial-tariff decomposition
G = E[L − C] + RI + CoC + EXP + M
G is the gross commercial rate: retained expected claims, reinsurance premium, capital cost, operating & loss-adjustment expense, and a governed margin — none of which the project currently has in a form that supports a binding tariff.
F = G × S
F is the farmer-paid rate after subsidy share S. A subsidy can lower the farmer rate without changing the technical estimate of risk — and should be shown explicitly rather than forced into the actuarial number itself.

Limitations that affect every rate in this paper

  • Claims and insured-exposure histories are not available in a form that can validate the current crop-level tariffs.
  • The V12.2 rice baseline covers only part of Punjab and excludes Sindh; two major Punjab rice raw histories remain behind an extraction gate.
  • Maize district yield is not split between spring and autumn — season-specific weather cannot replace missing season-specific yield.
  • Flood datasets have not been joined to crop exposure, insured area, depth, duration and crop stage at unit-area-of-insurance level.
  • Cotton pest alerts exist, but a matched scouting-treatment-yield or claims panel does not — the managed-cotton discount remains prior-led.
  • Actual reinsurance terms, capital standards, operating costs, loss-adjustment costs and a governed margin are not supplied.
  • The wheat 4.74% figure is a base climate-scenario benchmark, not an empirical climate tariff.
  • V12.2's climate-adjusted rates remain scenario indications; the historical/current state is the embedded V12.1 baseline, and the climate states are not binding tariffs.
A full tariff filing would still need to

Lock the data vintage and geographic boundary for each insurance unit; preserve the raw yield series and pre-season expected-yield calculation for every year; show annual payout ratios, pure loss cost, bootstrap interval and model-method envelope; show the credibility rule and any parent prior used for a thin local unit; show exposure weights and the treatment of unmodeled area; show reinsurance, capital, expense, loss-adjustment cost and margin as separate commercial items; and keep climate and pest overlays separate from the historical burn unless the model proves an incremental forward loss.
